Fe/MgO/Fe Geometry and Interface
Learning goals
By the end of this lesson, you should be able to explain the role of each region in a magnetic tunnel junction, distinguish an electrode from the central region, describe the Fe(001)/MgO(001) epitaxial relationship, estimate the in-plane lattice mismatch, and understand what the MgO barrier-layer count means in the QuantumATK MTJ Builder.
1. What is a magnetic tunnel junction?
A magnetic tunnel junction (MTJ) is a layered device that contains two ferromagnetic electrodes separated by a thin insulating barrier. For the model studied here:

Fe electrode | MgO insulating barrier | Fe electrode

The two Fe electrodes are ferromagnetic. MgO is an insulator and acts as the tunnel barrier. Electrons can cross the barrier by quantum tunneling even though the barrier is insulating in the usual bulk sense.

The current direction is perpendicular to the layers (CPP: current-perpendicular-to-plane). For the usual Fe(001)/MgO(001)/Fe(001) geometry, the interface planes are parallel to the x-y plane and transport is along z, normal to the interface.
2. The role of each region
Left and right Fe electrodes
The electrodes are the conducting ferromagnetic regions on either side of MgO. In a transport calculation, they represent reservoirs that extend away from the finite device region. They provide propagating electronic states and define the left and right contacts.
MgO barrier
MgO is the thin insulating region between the electrodes. The electronic wavefunction penetrates into the barrier and decays through it. The transmission generally becomes smaller as the barrier becomes thicker, although the detailed behaviour also depends on electronic symmetry, interface structure, energy, and bias.
Fe/MgO interfaces
There are two interfaces: left Fe/MgO and right MgO/Fe. At an interface, the bulk periodic arrangements of two different crystals meet. Atomic registry, bonding, interfacial distances, strain, and relaxation can influence the electronic states and the transmission. The interface is therefore not just a geometrical boundary; it is an important part of the physical device.
Central region and electrodes in a NEGF device
The central region is a finite atomistic region that contains the barrier and enough electrode-like material on both sides. The transport calculation connects this region to semi-infinite left and right electrodes. Do not confuse the finite Fe layers included near the barrier in the central region with the complete semi-infinite electrodes used by the transport formalism.
3. Why use (001) surfaces?
The notation (001) describes a crystallographic plane. In a cubic crystal, [001] is the direction normal to the (001) plane.

For the usual geometry:
- Fe(001) and MgO(001) planes form the interfaces;
- the x and y directions lie in the interface plane;
- the z direction is normal to the interface and is the transport direction.
A correct interface model must preserve a consistent in-plane periodicity across both materials.
4. Epitaxy and the 45-degree in-plane relationship
A commonly reported epitaxial relationship is:

Fe(001) || MgO(001)

Fe[100] || MgO[110]

The second relation means that the in-plane crystal axes are rotated by 45 degrees relative to one another when the two conventional cubic surface axes are compared. It allows a reasonably close match between the Fe and MgO surface repeat lengths.

Using representative bulk lattice parameters:
- a(Fe) = 2.8665 Å
- a(MgO) = 4.2115 Å
The MgO surface repeat length along its [110] direction, using the relevant FCC surface translation, is:

a(MgO) / √2 ≈ 2.978 Å

This is compared with the Fe(001) in-plane repeat length a(Fe) = 2.8665 Å.

If the Fe value is used as the reference, the magnitude of the mismatch is:

Mismatch = [2.978 − 2.8665] / 2.8665 × 100% ≈ 3.9%

Literature commonly reports a value around 3.8%; the small difference comes from the exact lattice constants and mismatch convention used.

This is not a perfect match. A coherent periodic interface requires a common in-plane cell, so one or both materials may be strained relative to their bulk lattice parameters. The selected strain convention must be stated and checked; do not assume a mismatch value alone uniquely determines the final cell.
5. What is a monolayer and how does barrier thickness matter?
A monolayer (ML) is commonly used to count atomic planes or repeated structural layers in a thin film. The exact convention must be checked in the paper or software being followed. For rock-salt MgO along [001], adjacent atomic planes are separated by approximately a(MgO)/2, about 2.11 Å, but authors may define the barrier layer count according to their chosen structural repeat or Builder convention.

The safest practice is to inspect the actual atomic coordinates and the total MgO thickness in the built structure rather than assuming that a field named “Number of layers” always means the same thing as “ML” in every publication.

As a physical trend, a thicker insulating barrier usually suppresses tunneling because the wavefunction decays through a longer barrier. In a simplified one-dimensional picture, transmission often has an approximately exponential dependence on thickness:

T ∝ exp(−2κd)

Here, d is the barrier thickness and κ is a decay constant determined by the barrier height and effective mass in the simplified model. This expression is qualitative; a real Fe/MgO/Fe junction also has symmetry-dependent and interface-dependent transport channels.
6. QuantumATK documentation — the FeMgO-style MTJ Builder
The official QuantumATK tutorial is titled Spin transport in magnetic tunnel junctions and is marked Version 2016.0. It describes a Fe|MgO|Fe magnetic tunnel junction and later spin-dependent transport calculations.

Tutorial: https://docs.quantumatk.com/tutorials/fe_mgo_fe/fe_mgo_fe.html

In the section Relaxing the device central region → Initial configuration, the tutorial gives this Builder path:

Add → From Plugin → Magnetic Tunnel Junction (FeMgO-style)

For its illustrative geometry, the tutorial sets:
- Number of barrier layers: 6
- Number of left surface layers: 2
- Number of right surface layers: 2
Then it asks the user to click Preview to inspect the geometry and Build to add it to the Stash. The tutorial next uses the Bulk tool to convert the device geometry into a periodic bulk central-region configuration by removing the electrodes.

Important: the values 6, 2, and 2 are the settings of the documentation example. They are not universal optimal values and should not automatically be treated as the settings for a new research model.
7. Why the central region is relaxed carefully
The official tutorial then sends the central region to the Script Generator and uses geometry optimization. It keeps the first and last four atoms in the central region fixed because they belong to the electrode extensions. If these atoms move, the periodic structure at the edges may no longer match the bulk electrodes that will later be attached.

The tutorial uses this workflow to preserve bulk-like periodicity at the edges while allowing the interface region to relax. The details of the calculator settings belong to the later DFT lesson; do not copy the tutorial's values blindly into a research calculation without convergence tests.
8. QuantumATK Builder practice for Day 02
1. Open the Builder in QuantumATK 2016.4.
2. Select Add → From Plugin → Magnetic Tunnel Junction (FeMgO-style).
3. Inspect the available fields and hover over them for tooltips.
4. For a first reproduction of the documentation example, enter 6 barrier layers, 2 left surface layers, and 2 right surface layers.
5. Click Preview. Rotate the structure and identify the sequence Fe | MgO | Fe.
6. Check which direction is the transport direction and which directions are periodic in the interface plane.

For this lesson, do not run DFT or NEGF. We are learning the geometry and the meaning of the Builder fields first.
